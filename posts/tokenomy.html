Following a Dollar Through the Tokenomy

How the neocloud and token-selling businesses work, who keeps how much of each dollar along the way, and why every layer can look profitable at the same time.

TL;DR
  • The AI economy runs on two units. Infrastructure companies sell GPU-hours and model companies sell tokens, and most other businesses mark up one of the two.
  • One estimate puts about half of each dollar of token revenue with the model company, about 13¢ with cloud and data centers and about 13¢ with the GPU vendor, leaving a few cents each for memory, power and foundries (Shin, 2026).
  • Reported margins differ widely by layer. Nvidia's gross margin is above 70%, neoclouds report about 56% adjusted EBITDA but close to zero operating margin, and model labs reportedly sit near 33 to 40% gross margin.
  • Much of the money moves in a loop. Chip vendors invest in labs and backstop clouds, labs sign large compute contracts, and clouds borrow against those contracts to buy chips. This is mostly ordinary vendor financing, but it concentrates risk.
  • This month the market is testing the weakest link, with CoreWeave's bonds yielding around 15% and lenders pulling back.

A year ago "neocloud" was industry jargon. This week it is in the business headlines, after CoreWeave, the largest of the new GPU clouds, lost 15% of its market value in a day when a chart of its bond yield spread on social media (Edwards, 2026). Behind the headlines is a basic question that turns out to be hard to answer. When someone pays for an AI answer, where does the money go, and does anyone along the way make a profit?

In this post I try to answer it with public filings and the best reporting I could find. The numbers are approximate, because much of the industry is private and many figures are leaks reported secondhand. I note the source for each one.

01AI is sold in two units

Almost every business in the stack sells one of two things.

  • GPU-hours. One accelerator rented for one hour. Hyperscalers such as AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and Oracle sell them, and so do neoclouds such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda and Crusoe, either on demand or through multi-year contracts.
  • Tokens. Pieces of text going into or out of a model, priced per million. Model labs such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google sell them, as do platforms that host open models, such as Together and Fireworks.

Turning GPU-hours into tokens is the central technical step in the industry, and I use "tokenomy" as shorthand for the economy built around it. The conversion rate, measured in tokens per GPU-hour, depends on the model, the hardware and how well requests are batched. Much of the economics comes down to who controls that rate and who carries its risk.

02Every token passes through a long supply chain

Consider a single request. A developer using Cursor asks for a refactor, and Cursor sends the prompt to a model, either through the lab's own API or through a marketplace such as AWS Bedrock, Azure or OpenRouter. The lab runs the model on accelerators rented from a cloud, and the cloud bought those chips from Nvidia or AMD. The chips were made at TSMC with memory from SK hynix or Micron, then installed in a data center that draws a great deal of power. Money moves down this chain and the service moves back up.

Three features of this chain matter more than they first appear to.

  • The pricing unit changes at each boundary. The user pays a flat subscription, the app pays per token, the lab pays per GPU-hour and the cloud pays per chip. Each change of unit is a point where someone takes on risk and gets paid for it.
  • Marketplaces keep a share. When a lab's model is sold through a cloud marketplace, the cloud keeps part of the revenue. Until this year Microsoft passed only 20% of its Azure revenue from OpenAI models back to OpenAI. Under the restructured agreement Microsoft stops paying that share, while OpenAI keeps paying Microsoft 20% of its revenue through 2030, now with a cap (Bloomberg, 2026). Reuters estimated that Anthropic pays its cloud partners roughly 16¢ of every dollar it earns through them.
  • The lab often does not own its GPUs. This is the reason neoclouds exist, and the reason their results depend on a handful of labs.

03Most of each dollar stays with the model company

The clearest public attempt I found to split one dollar of token revenue comes from Korea Investment & Securities (Shin, 2026). The analysis separates revenue attribution, which tracks who sends the invoice, from value added, which is the surplus each layer keeps once double counting is removed. Value added is the better measure of who earns what.

The model company keeps the largest share of value per dollar, but that does not make it the most profitable layer. Labs carry the heaviest fixed costs in training runs, research salaries and free users, so a large share of gross value can still end in an operating loss. iCapital describes foundation-model companies as running "at an operating loss, funded by external capital rather than revenue," and finds the largest profit pools in hardware (iCapital, 2026).

The upstream layers keep a small share of each token dollar, at high margins, and they are paid first. Nvidia is paid when the chip ships, while the lab earns back that cost token by token over several years. A company's position on this payment timeline matters about as much as its margin.

Here is a short description of each layer.

  • Chips. Nvidia reported a 71.1% GAAP gross margin for fiscal 2026 and 75.0% in its most recent quarter, on $62.3B of data center revenue in that quarter (Nvidia, 2026). It is the most profitable layer, and as Section 6 shows, it also finances much of the rest.
  • Neoclouds. They borrow to buy GPUs and rent them out by the hour. EBITDA is strong and operating profit is thin, for reasons Section 4 explains.
  • Hyperscalers. They rent GPUs too, but they also own the customer relationship, sell their own models and chips and take a cut of marketplace sales. They are the most diversified layer and also the largest spenders, with combined 2026 capex for the big four estimated near $725B (Shin, 2026).
  • Model labs. OpenAI's adjusted gross margin reportedly fell to about 33% in 2025 from about 40%, below its 46% target, as inference costs roughly quadrupled to $8.4B. Anthropic's was projected near 40%, also below target (BigGo, 2026, summarizing The Information).
  • Inference platforms. Companies that host open models compete on optimization. Analyst estimates put Together AI's gross margin near 45% and Fireworks near 50% (Sacra). Routers such as OpenRouter keep a few percent of the spend that passes through them.
  • Apps. For apps, tokens are the cost of goods sold, so cheaper tokens improve their margins. iCapital expects application companies to benefit most as inference prices fall (iCapital, 2026), and investors already price them that way. Perplexity trades at about 222 times estimated gross profit, compared with about 24 times for Together AI (Tunguz, 2025).

Venture investors and consultants draw this kind of map often, usually with three layers for infrastructure, models and applications (a16z, 2023). I split it into eight layers so that it lines up with Figure 1. The right side shows the four large technology companies that now run products in almost every layer, which is the biggest structural change since that three-layer picture was drawn.

Concentration varies a lot from layer to layer. Nvidia sells most AI accelerators, and a handful of labs earn most model revenue. Menlo Ventures estimated at the end of 2025 that Anthropic held about 40% of enterprise LLM API spend, OpenAI about 27% and Google about 21% (Menlo Ventures, 2025). The cloud and inference layers are far more crowded, which helps explain why their prices fell fastest.

04Neoclouds live on the spread between rent and cost

A neocloud borrows money, buys GPUs and rents them out for more than they cost to own. The model is simple, but its results depend heavily on three numbers, which are the rental price, utilization and the useful life of a GPU.

The price has fallen quickly. H100 rentals were close to $8 an hour at the late-2024 peak. By December 2025 they cost about $2.85 to $3.50 on demand and about $1.90 to $2.15 on one-year commitments at the large clouds (Introl, 2025). More than 300 new providers entered the market in 2025, and demand shifted from training large models toward inference, which is more sensitive to price.

Long contracts soften the decline. CoreWeave says 98% of its revenue comes from committed take-or-pay contracts. It reports $103.7B in remaining performance obligations, meaning revenue that is contracted but not yet recognized, and its top three customers produced 72% of second-quarter revenue (Edwards, 2026). Microsoft alone accounted for 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue, according to its IPO filing.

Depreciation decides whether the business makes money. In the first quarter of 2026, depreciation and amortization equaled 55% of CoreWeave's revenue and 53% of Nebius's (Daloopa, 2026). CoreWeave depreciates its GPUs over six years and Nebius over four. To see why that single assumption matters so much, I built a simple model of one H100.

The toy model matches what analysts find in the reported numbers. Kerrisdale's model of CoreWeave's GB200 fleet shows an EBIT margin of about 20% under a six-year life and 87% utilization, falling to near zero with a four- or five-year life (cited in Luminix, 2026). CoreWeave's adjusted operating margin fell from 19% in 2024 to 13% in 2025 and 1% in the first quarter of 2026 as new fleets began to depreciate (Daloopa, 2026). Debt-financed clusters reportedly need about 70% utilization just to cover depreciation and interest.

So the EBITDA of a neocloud is real, but its profit depends on an estimate of how many years a GPU keeps earning, and the debt has to be repaid on a fixed schedule either way.

05Labs earn their margin converting GPU-hours into tokens

One level up, a lab buys GPU-hours and sells tokens, and its gross margin can be approximated with a short formula.

gross margin ≈ 1 − (cost per GPU-hour) ÷ (tokens per GPU-hour × price per token)

Each lever in the token business moves one of the three terms in that formula.

  • Tokens per GPU-hour depend on engineering. Batching many users onto one GPU, caching shared prompts, speculative decoding, quantization and distilling large models into smaller ones all raise the number. Platforms such as Fireworks charge a premium for doing this well.
  • Price per token depends on segmentation. a16z found that the price of a fixed level of capability falls about tenfold each year (Appenzeller, 2024), and iCapital notes that GPT-3-level performance went from about $60 to about $0.06 per million tokens between 2021 and 2024. Frontier models do not follow that curve, because labs keep a premium tier and a cheap tier and price them very differently.
  • Cost per GPU-hour depends on procurement. Long contracts are cheaper than last-minute capacity, and OpenAI's 2025 margin shortfall was partly attributed to buying expensive compute at short notice.

Free users also weigh on margins. Reportedly about 95% of ChatGPT users do not pay, and their tokens are a real cost. This helps explain why gross margin at a consumer lab is much lower than the API economics alone would suggest.

Falling prices do not necessarily mean falling revenue, because reasoning models and agents use far more tokens per task. One investor framework found that hidden reasoning made up between a third and nearly 60% of the tokens in a legal-analysis workload. It is still unclear whether cheaper tokens or higher volume will win out.

06Much of the money starts at the bottom of the stack

Figure 1 leaves out the most unusual feature of the tokenomy. A good share of the money that flows down the stack started near the bottom.

The arrows in Figure 5 form a loop. Nvidia invests in OpenAI, OpenAI commits hundreds of billions of dollars to Oracle and CoreWeave, and those clouds borrow against the contracts to buy Nvidia GPUs. Nvidia also owns part of CoreWeave and has agreed to buy up to $6.3B of any capacity CoreWeave cannot sell, through April 2032. The same underlying spending appears as an investment, as contract backlog and as chip revenue.

Most careful commentators do not treat this as fraud. Noah Smith argues that it resembles ordinary vendor financing, much like a carmaker lending customers money to buy its cars. Revenue really does move in one direction, and it is reasonable for a cash-rich supplier to support a customer with less access to capital. He also points out that the deals tie these companies together, lowering each one's individual risk while raising the system's exposure to an AI downturn (Smith, 2025). Money also returns to the model companies by a quieter route. Venture capital funds apps, the apps spend that money on tokens, and the purchases show up as lab revenue.

07Every layer can report a profit while the system loses money

Taken together, these pieces present a puzzle. Nearly every company in the chain reports a healthy-looking margin, yet the system as a whole spends far more than it earns. Four mechanisms explain the gap.

  • Different measures. Neoclouds lead with adjusted EBITDA, labs with gross margin and chipmakers with GAAP gross margin. Each figure is accurate, and none of them is comparable with the others.
  • Accounting estimates. Choosing a six-year or a four-year GPU life is the difference between profit and loss in Figure 4. Meta's move to a 5.5-year life for servers suggests that even the hyperscalers are still estimating.
  • Backlog counted before cash. Remaining performance obligations look like future revenue, but they depend on the customer still being able to pay in 2029.
  • Outside capital at the top. The final buyer is often funded by investors rather than by customers, so each layer below records real revenue from money that began as a funding round.

Sequoia's David Cahn proposed a system-level check in 2024. He took Nvidia's data center revenue run rate, doubled it to cover the rest of the data center, and doubled it again to leave a margin for the companies selling to end users. The result was about $600B in annual revenue that the industry would need to earn (Cahn, 2024). The 2026 Korea Invest analysis describes the same gap as an inverted pyramid, with roughly $60B to $100B of annual model and app revenue resting on about $300B of GPU revenue and about $725B of hyperscaler capex (Shin, 2026). The industry is building capacity for the demand it expects rather than the demand it has. Railways and fiber networks were built the same way, and in both cases the risk lay in the timing.

08The press turned skeptical this month

Below is recent coverage, roughly in the order I would read it.

  • Fortune (Oct 16, 2026) presents the bull case for CoreWeave from one of its investors, who argues that noncancelable take-or-pay contracts with creditworthy customers support the debt. The same article lists the figures that worry others, including $35.6B of debt, $16.3B of leases and a BB+ rating, and cites Gartner's estimate that 80% of neoclouds will stop operating independently by 2030 (Edwards, 2026).
  • CNBC (Oct 14, 2026) reports traders buying "bubble puts" on CoreWeave as neocloud stocks fall.
  • Axios (Oct 16, 2026) reports that banks are pulling back. Citing the FT, it says global AI-related debt issuance fell to $23B in September from $113B in June.
  • Bloomberg published the most complete visual map of the circular deals and separately reported the end of Microsoft's revenue share to OpenAI.
  • iCapital and Korea Invest (both July 2026) offer the two most useful explanations of the whole stack, one by profit pool and the other by value added per dollar.
  • Noah Smith (Oct 2025) makes the clearest case that the circular deals are vendor financing rather than fraud, while still representing a systemic risk.
  • SemiAnalysis published "AI Neocloud Playbook and Anatomy" in 2024. It remains the most detailed operator-level analysis of neocloud costs, though it is behind a paywall.
  • Sequoia's "AI's $600B Question" (2024) is the original statement of the revenue gap.

09A few numbers will decide how this plays out

  • GPU useful life. If H100s are still earning money in 2029, the six-year schedules were right and the neoclouds will be fine. If newer chips make them uneconomic sooner, the losses will arrive all at once.
  • Utilization and spot prices. Contracts protect the largest players, so the spot market gives the earliest warning. It is worth watching whether one-year commitment prices keep diverging from on-demand prices.
  • The refinancing wall. Tens of billions of dollars of GPU-backed loans at CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda and Crusoe come due between 2026 and 2028, and this month's bond market offers a preview.
  • Price per token against tokens per task. If agents and reasoning keep raising token volume faster than prices fall, revenue at the top of the pyramid will grow into the capacity below it.
  • End-user revenue. Every margin in this post is ultimately paid out of what businesses and consumers will spend on AI. That number is growing quickly, and the open question is whether it grows quickly enough.

I don't think the tokenomy is a Ponzi scheme, and I don't think it is a sure thing either. It is a supply chain built ahead of its demand, financed partly by its own suppliers, with an accounting estimate holding up the middle. I will update this post as new numbers come in.
