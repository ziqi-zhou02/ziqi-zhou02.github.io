Tokenomy: Following a Dollar from GPU to Token

How the neocloud and token-selling businesses actually work: who touches a token on its way to you, who keeps how much of your dollar, and why every layer can look profitable at the same time.

TL;DR
  • The AI economy runs on two units. Infrastructure sells GPU-hours; model companies sell tokens. Everything else is a markup between the two.
  • One estimate of where a dollar of token revenue ends up: roughly half stays with the model company, about 13¢ goes to cloud and data centers, about 13¢ to the GPU vendor, and only a few cents each to memory, power and foundries (Korea Invest, 2026).
  • Reported margins look very different by layer. Nvidia's gross margin is above 70%. Neoclouds post ~56% adjusted EBITDA but near-zero operating margin after depreciation. Model labs reportedly sit at ~33–40% gross margin.
  • A large share of the money is circular. Chip vendors invest in labs and backstop clouds, labs sign giant compute contracts, and clouds borrow against those contracts to buy chips. This is mostly ordinary vendor financing, but it concentrates risk.
  • As of this month the market is testing the weakest link: CoreWeave's bonds yield ~15% and lenders are pulling back.

A year ago "neocloud" was industry jargon. This week it's on the front page of the business section. CoreWeave, the largest of the new GPU clouds, saw its stock drop 15% in a day after a chart of its bond yield went viral (Edwards, 2026). Underneath the headlines is a simple question that's surprisingly hard to answer: when you pay for an AI answer, where does the money go, and does anyone along the way actually make a profit?

This post tries to answer it from first principles, using public filings and the best reporting I could find. Treat the numbers as approximate: much of this industry is private, and many figures come from leaks reported secondhand. I've tried to say where each number comes from.

01Two units: GPU-hours and tokens

Almost every business in the stack sells one of two things.

  • GPU-hours. One accelerator, rented for one hour. Hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle) and neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe) sell these, either on demand or through multi-year contracts.
  • Tokens. A chunk of text in or out of a model, priced per million. Model labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) sell these, as do inference platforms that host open models (Together, Fireworks).

Turning GPU-hours into tokens is the core technical act of the industry, and "tokenomy" is my shorthand for the economy built around it. The conversion rate, tokens per GPU-hour, depends on the model, the hardware and how cleverly requests are batched. Most of the interesting economics comes down to who controls that conversion rate and who carries its risk.

02The life of a token

Follow one request. A developer in Cursor asks for a refactor. Cursor sends the prompt to a model, either directly through the lab's API or through a marketplace like AWS Bedrock, Azure or OpenRouter. The lab runs the model on accelerators it rents from a cloud. That cloud bought the chips from Nvidia or AMD, which had them fabbed at TSMC with memory from SK hynix or Micron, and racked them in a data center that needs a lot of power. Money flows down that chain, and the service flows back up.

Three details matter more than they first appear to:

  • Pricing units change at each boundary. The user pays a flat subscription, the app pays per token, the lab pays per GPU-hour, and the cloud pays per chip. Each change of unit is a place where someone takes on risk, and is paid for it.
  • Marketplaces take a cut. When a lab's model is sold through a cloud marketplace, the cloud keeps a share. Until this year Microsoft paid OpenAI only 20% of what it earned reselling OpenAI models on Azure. Under the restructured deal Microsoft no longer pays that share, while OpenAI keeps paying Microsoft 20% of its revenue through 2030, now capped (Bloomberg, 2026). Reuters estimated that Anthropic pays its cloud partners roughly 16¢ of every dollar it earns through them.
  • The lab often doesn't own the GPUs. That single fact is why neoclouds exist, and why their fortunes are tied to a handful of labs.

03The value chain: who keeps what

The cleanest public attempt to split one dollar of token revenue I found comes from Korea Investment & Securities (Shin, 2026). The analysis separates revenue attribution (who sends the invoice) from value added (the surplus each layer keeps once you remove double counting). Value added is the right lens for "who earns what."

Two things stand out. First, the model company keeps the largest share of value per dollar. That isn't the same as being the most profitable. Labs carry the heaviest fixed costs (training runs, research salaries, free users), so a large slice of gross value can still mean an operating loss. iCapital puts it bluntly: foundation-model companies "run at an operating loss, funded by external capital rather than revenue," while the hardware vendors capture the largest profit pools (iCapital, 2026).

Second, the upstream layers keep a small share per token dollar but with very high margins. They are also paid up front. Nvidia sells the chip on day one; the lab only recovers that cost token by token over years. Where you sit on the payment timeline matters as much as your margin.

Here is each layer in one line:

  • Chips (Nvidia, AMD). Nvidia reported a 71.1% GAAP gross margin for fiscal 2026 and 75.0% in its latest quarter, on $62.3B of data center revenue in that quarter (Nvidia, 2026). This is the most profitable layer, and the one financing much of the rest (see §6).
  • Neoclouds. A spread business: buy GPUs with debt, rent them by the hour. Strong EBITDA, thin operating profit. More in §4.
  • Hyperscalers. They rent GPUs too, but they also own the customer relationship, sell their own models and chips, and take marketplace cuts. They are the best diversified, and also the biggest spenders: the big four's 2026 capex is estimated near $725B (Shin, 2026).
  • Model labs. OpenAI's adjusted gross margin reportedly fell to ~33% in 2025 from ~40%, below its 46% target, as inference costs roughly quadrupled to $8.4B. Anthropic's was projected around 40%, also below target (The Information, as summarized by BigGo, 2026).
  • Inference platforms. Hosts of open models live on optimization. Analyst estimates put Together AI's gross margin near 45% and Fireworks near 50% (Sacra). Routers like OpenRouter take a few percent on spend that passes through them.
  • Apps. Tokens are their cost of goods. Cheaper tokens mean better margins, which is why application companies are often described as the natural winners when inference prices fall (iCapital, 2026). Investors agree: Perplexity trades at ~222× estimated gross profit, compared with ~24× for Together AI (Tunguz, 2025).

04Inside a neocloud: the spread business

A neocloud's business fits in a sentence: borrow money, buy GPUs, rent them out for more than they cost to own. The spread is the business. It sounds simple, but it's sensitive to three numbers: the rental price, utilization, and how long a GPU stays useful.

The price fell fast. H100 rentals were near $8/hour at the peak in late 2024. By December 2025 they were ~$2.85–3.50 on demand and ~$1.90–2.15 on one-year commitments at the big clouds (Introl, 2025). More than 300 new providers entered in 2025 alone. Demand also shifted from training giant models to inference, which is more price-sensitive.

Contracts are the cushion. CoreWeave says 98% of its revenue comes from committed take-or-pay contracts. It reports $103.7B of remaining performance obligations (signed but unrecognized revenue), and its top three customers made up 72% of Q2 revenue (Edwards, 2026). Microsoft alone was 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue, per its IPO filing.

Depreciation is the whole game. In Q1 2026, depreciation and amortization was 55% of CoreWeave's revenue and 53% of Nebius's (Daloopa, 2026). CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over six years; Nebius uses four. To see why that one assumption matters so much, I built a toy model of one H100.

The toy model reproduces what analysts see in the real numbers. Kerrisdale's model of CoreWeave's GB200 fleet shows a ~20% EBIT margin under its six-year depreciation life and 87% utilization, falling to near zero at four to five years (cited in Luminix, 2026). CoreWeave's adjusted operating margin went from 19% in 2024 to 13% in 2025 to 1% in Q1 2026 as new fleets started depreciating (Daloopa, 2026). Debt-financed clusters reportedly need around 70% utilization just to cover depreciation and interest.

So the honest summary of the neocloud model is: EBITDA is real, profit depends on an accounting estimate, namely how many years a GPU keeps earning, and the debt has to be repaid on a fixed schedule regardless.

05Inside the token business

One level up, the lab buys GPU-hours and sells tokens. Its gross margin is roughly:

gross margin ≈ 1 − (cost per GPU-hour) ÷ (tokens per GPU-hour × price per token)

Every lever in the token business moves one of those three terms:

  • Tokens per GPU-hour is engineering: batching many users onto one GPU, caching shared prompts, speculative decoding, quantization, and distilling big models into smaller ones. This is where inference platforms like Fireworks earn their premium.
  • Price per token is segmentation. a16z found that the price of a fixed level of capability falls roughly 10× per year ("LLMflation"), and iCapital notes that GPT-3-level performance went from ~$60 to ~$0.06 per million tokens between 2021 and 2024. Frontier models don't follow that curve: labs keep a premium tier and a cheap tier, and price them very differently.
  • Cost per GPU-hour is procurement: long contracts are cheaper than last-minute capacity. OpenAI's 2025 margin miss was partly blamed on buying expensive compute at short notice to meet demand.

Then there are the non-paying users. Reportedly around 95% of ChatGPT users don't pay. Their tokens are a real cost, which is one reason "gross margin" at a consumer lab is so much lower than the API economics alone would suggest.

Finally, falling prices don't necessarily mean falling revenue. Reasoning models and agents burn far more tokens per task. One investor framework found hidden reasoning was a third to nearly 60% of tokens in a legal-analysis workload. Cheaper tokens, more of them: the question is which effect wins.

06How the money circles back

If you only drew Fig. 1, you'd miss the strangest part of the tokenomy: a lot of the money flowing down the stack started at the bottom.

Read the arrows as a loop. Nvidia invests in OpenAI. OpenAI commits hundreds of billions to Oracle and CoreWeave. Those clouds borrow against the contracts to buy Nvidia GPUs. Nvidia also owns part of CoreWeave and has agreed to buy up to $6.3B of any capacity CoreWeave can't sell, through April 2032. The same underlying spending shows up as an investment, a contract backlog, and chip revenue.

Is this a scam? Most careful commentators say no. Noah Smith argues it looks like ordinary vendor financing, like a carmaker lending customers money to buy its cars. Revenue really does flow one way, and it makes sense for a cash-rich supplier to help a capital-constrained customer. But he also argues that it ties everyone's fortunes together: the deals reduce each company's individual risk while increasing the system's exposure to an AI downturn (Smith, 2025). The money also flows back to model companies in a quieter way. Venture capital funds apps, apps spend it on tokens, and those token purchases show up as lab revenue.

07Why every layer looks profitable

Put the pieces together and you get a puzzle. Nearly every company in the chain reports something that looks like a healthy margin, yet the system as a whole is spending far more than it earns. Four mechanisms reconcile the two:

  • Different yardsticks. Neoclouds lead with adjusted EBITDA (~56%), labs with gross margin, and chipmakers with GAAP gross margin. Each figure is true and none of them is comparable.
  • Accounting estimates. A six-year GPU life versus a four-year life is the difference between profit and loss (Fig. 3). Meta's move to a 5.5-year life for servers shows that even hyperscalers are still guessing.
  • Backlog counted before cash. Remaining performance obligations look like future revenue, but they depend on the customer still being able to pay in 2029.
  • External capital at the top. The final buyer is often funded by investors, not customers. Every layer below books real revenue from money that started as someone's funding round.

The system-level check is the one Sequoia's David Cahn proposed in 2024. Take Nvidia's data-center run rate, double it to cover the rest of the data center, then double again to leave the end user's vendor a margin. That gave a ~$600B annual revenue bar the industry needed to clear (Cahn, 2024). Korea Invest's 2026 version makes the same point as an "inverted pyramid": roughly $60–100B of annual model and app revenue sitting on top of ~$300B of GPU run-rate revenue and ~$725B of hyperscaler capex (Shin, 2026). The industry is building capacity for demand it expects, not demand it has. That's not necessarily wrong, since railways and fiber were built the same way, but it means the timing carries the risk.

08What the press is saying right now

A quick map of recent coverage, in roughly the order I'd read it:

  • Fortune (Oct 16, 2026) lays out the bull case for CoreWeave from one of its investors: noncancelable take-or-pay contracts with high-credit customers back the debt. Alongside it are the numbers that worry people: $35.6B of debt plus $16.3B of leases, and a BB+ rating. Gartner expects 80% of neoclouds to stop operating independently by 2030 (Edwards, 2026).
  • CNBC (Oct 14, 2026) reports traders piling into "bubble puts" on CoreWeave as neocloud stocks slide.
  • Axios (Oct 16, 2026) reports banks pulling back. Citing the FT, it says global AI-related debt issuance fell to $23B in September from $113B in June.
  • Bloomberg built the definitive visual of the circular deals, and separately reported the end of Microsoft's revenue share to OpenAI.
  • iCapital (Jul 2026) and Korea Invest (Jul 2026) are the two best "whole-stack" explainers: profit pools by layer, and value added per dollar.
  • Noah Smith (Oct 2025) is the clearest argument that circular deals are vendor financing, not fraud, while still being a systemic risk.
  • SemiAnalysis's "AI Neocloud Playbook and Anatomy" (2024) is still the deepest operator-level teardown of neocloud cost of ownership (paywalled).
  • Sequoia's "AI's $600B Question" (2024) is the original framing of the revenue gap.

09What to watch

  • GPU useful life. If H100s are still earning money in 2029, the six-year schedules were right and neoclouds are fine. If Blackwell and its successors make them uneconomic sooner, the losses show up all at once.
  • Utilization and spot prices. Contracts protect the big players, but the spot market is the early warning. Watch whether one-year commitment prices keep diverging from on-demand prices.
  • The refinancing wall. Tens of billions of GPU-backed loans across CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda and Crusoe come due in 2026–2028. This month's bond market is a preview.
  • Price per token vs. tokens per task. If agents and reasoning keep multiplying token volume faster than prices fall, the top of the pyramid fills in.
  • End-user revenue. In the end, every margin in this post is paid out of what businesses and consumers are willing to pay for answers. That number is growing fast. The question is whether it grows fast enough.

The tokenomy is not a Ponzi scheme, and it's not a sure thing. It's a supply chain being built ahead of its demand, financed by its own suppliers, with an accounting estimate holding up the middle. That's a fascinating thing to watch. I'll update this post as the numbers come in.
